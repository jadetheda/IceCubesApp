# Style Guidelines for Documentation & AI-Text Detection Guide

The purpose of this document is two-fold. First, it establishes the standard rules and guidelines for writing documentation within this project to avoid the pitfalls of modern "AI-slop"—the recognizable, generic, and uninspired text commonly generated by large language models. Secondly, it serves as a comprehensive, multi-source reference guide on how to identify AI-generated writing. This knowledge is derived from extensive research and should be applied directly to our user-facing and internal documentation (like `README.md`, `memory.md`, and `notes_and_lessons.md`).

By understanding how AI sounds, we can purposefully author, edit, and curate our project documentation and application UI text to sound strictly human, authentic, and professionally straightforward.

---

## Part I: How to Identify AI-Generated Writing

AI-generated text is ubiquitous. With the rise of advanced conversational models, spotting machine-written content has become a crucial skill for editors, educators, and developers aiming to maintain authenticity. While AI models are highly capable, they rely on statistical patterns, probability, and vast amounts of training data. This underlying architecture results in specific linguistic "tells." Researching five primary aspects—lexical choices, syntactic structure, lack of depth, structural rigidity, and emotional flatlining—reveals exactly how AI gives itself away.

### 1. Lexical Tells: The over-reliance on “Transition Words” and "Flowery Vocabulary"

The most immediate red flag in AI-generated writing is vocabulary. AI models are programmed to produce highly cohesive, broadly acceptable, and technically "correct" text. To ensure readability and logical flow, they aggressively rely on transition words and specific adjectives.

**The "In Conclusion" Effect**
AI models are heavily fine-tuned to signal the structure of their arguments. In human writing, a concluding thought is often integrated naturally. In AI writing, almost every summarized section begins with "In conclusion," "Ultimately," "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally," or "Consequently." The sheer density of these connective adverbs is a mathematical artifact of the model predicting the safest way to bridge two related concepts. When every paragraph begins with a transition word, the text begins to sound robotic and formulaic.

**The "Delve" and "Tapestry" Phenomenon**
Certain words have seen a massive spike in usage since 2023, serving as almost definite markers of AI generation. Why? Because during the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) phase of model training, human graders highly rated text that sounded "sophisticated." Models learned that using words like "delve," "tapestry," "multifaceted," "nuance," "bustling," "testament," "crucial," and "pivotal" resulted in higher scores. 

For example, an AI describing a city won't say it's busy; it will say "the bustling metropolis is a tapestry of multifaceted cultures." It won't say we should look into a topic; it will say "let us delve into the nuances of this crucial paradigm." Humans rarely speak or write this way unless consciously attempting to sound academic. When these words appear repeatedly in casual, technical, or instructional documentation, it immediately sounds unnatural.

**The Absence of Idioms and Slang (or Awkward Use Thereof)**
Humans naturally weave in and out of formal and informal speech. We use idioms, regional phrasing, and culturally specific shorthand. AI avoids slang because it is statistically safer to use standard English. If prompted to use slang, the AI often misapplies it or surrounds the casual phrase with overly formal sentence structures, creating a jarring juxtaposition.

### 2. Syntactic Structure: The "Perfect" and Predictable Sentence

Human writing is messy. We write in fragments, we use run-on sentences when excited, and we heavily vary our sentence lengths based on the rhythm of the narrative. AI models, conversely, optimize for perfect grammar and predictable cadence.

**Uniform Sentence Length**
A concept known in linguistics as "burstiness" refers to the variation in sentence length and complexity. Human writing has high burstiness: a ten-word sentence. Followed by a three-word sentence. Then, suddenly, a sprawling, complex, multi-clause sentence that runs for lines on end because the author is trying to capture a complex, cascading thought. 

AI writing has incredibly low burstiness. Almost every sentence is 15 to 25 words long. They usually feature a subject, a verb, an object, and a modifying clause. This uniform rhythm creates a hypnotic, droning effect for the reader. When you read a document where every sentence feels mathematically balanced, you are likely reading AI.

**The lack of "Negative Space"**
Humans will often state a plain fact and move on. "Click the button." AI generally struggles with brevity unless heavily constrained. Because models are designed to be "helpful," they suffer from verbose over-explanation. "Click the primary submission button located at the bottom of the interface to complete the process and save your settings." identifying AI often involves simply noticing this lack of syntactic "negative space"—the inability to just say the thing and stop talking.

### 3. Structural Rigidity: The 5-Paragraph Essay Syndrome

AI models excel at producing structured text, often to their detriment. When asked a complex question, the default output usually mimics a high-school 5-paragraph essay or a corporate memo.

**The Sandbox Introduction**
AI loves to start by stating the premise of what it is about to do, or giving a broad, universally agreed-upon platitude. "In today's fast paced digital landscape, translating software is more important than ever." Humans writing technical documentation usually just start the documentation. AI must ramp up to the topic.

**Symmetrical Body Paragraphs**
The body of AI text is often broken down into neat, symmetrical lists or bullet points. Every bullet point will have roughly the same amount of text. The structure is mathematically fair to every point. Humans, on the other hand, care more about some points than others. A human might write three paragraphs on the crucial first step, and a single sentence for the second step. AI will attempt to write exactly one paragraph for every step to maintain structural symmetry.

**The Summarizing Conclusion**
Even when completely unnecessary—such as at the end of a brief troubleshooting guide—an AI will often append a concluding paragraph summarizing what was just said. "By following these steps, you can ensure that your ROM is properly patched and ready for use." It is a hallmark of current LLM training that the model must neatly wrap a bow on the conversation before gracefully exiting.

### 4. Lack of Depth and "Hallucinated" Generalities

When humans write from experience, they include hyper-specific, sometimes irrelevant details that ground the narrative in reality. An AI does not possess genuine lived experience, nor does it have an innate conceptual model of physical reality; it predicts the next statistically likely word.

**The "Gliding Over" Problem**
If you ask an AI to describe troubleshooting a specific PS1 emulator bug, it will give a perfectly formatted response that sounds plausible. However, upon closer inspection, the steps are often generalized abstractions. "Ensure your software is updated," "Check the configuration settings," "Consult the manual." 

A human who actually solved the bug would say: "Make sure you uncheck the weird 'HLE BIOS' box hidden in the audio settings tab, because for some reason it breaks the text rendering in this specific game." To spot AI writing, look for text that sounds authoritative but provides absolutely no actionable, concrete specifics. It glides over the granular reality of the problem in favor of broad, structurally sound generalities.

**Fence-Sitting and Lack of Voice**
AI models are aligned to be harmless, unbiased, and objective. This means they rarely take a strong, controversial, or highly opinionated stance unless forced. Their writing is sterilized. In technical documentation, a human developer might write, "Do not use Library X, it's garbage for memory management and will crash the server." An AI will write, "While Library X is a popular choice, developers should carefully consider its memory management implications in high-throughput environments." The lack of an authorial "edge" or distinct personality is a glaring sign of machine generation.

### 5. Emotional Flatlining

Genuine human emotion in writing is derived from context, stakes, and personal investment. Emotional AI writing is derived from a simulation of what emotional text *looks* like. 

**Forced Empathy and Over-Apologizing**
In customer service scenarios or conversational interfaces, AI models often overcompensate with aggressive politeness and forced empathy. "I completely understand how frustrating it must be to experience a bug in the localization tool." This simulated bedside manner feels hollow because it is applied universally, regardless of the severity of the user's issue. The emotional affect remains completely static at a 7/10 helpfulness baseline throughout the entire document, whereas human emotional tone fluctuates wildly depending on the writer's actual state of mind.

## Part II: Applying this to Our Project Documentation

To ensure our project documentation (`README.md`, `memory.md`, `notes_and_lessons.md`, etc.) remains pristine, accessible, and human-readable, we must actively strip out the AI tells detailed above. 

This project requires high-quality, dense technical documentation. The following style guidelines MUST be followed whenever new documentation is written or existing documentation is updated:

### 1. Ban the "AI Vocabulary"
You are strictly forbidden from using the following words in any documentation unless technically required by a proper noun:
* Delve
* Tapestry
* Multifaceted
* Seamless (e.g., "seamless integration")
* Robust
* Bustling
* Testament (e.g., "is a testament to")
* Crucial / Vital / Pivotal (use them sparingly, if at all)
* Furthermore / Moreover / Additionally (in excess)
* "In conclusion"

### 2. Embrace Asymmetry and Brevity
* **Stop over-explaining.** If an instruction is "Run the script," do not write "Execute the script to initiate the patching process." Just write "Run the script."
* **Break structural symmetry.** If a point only needs one sentence, give it one sentence. Do not pad sections with filler text just to make the document look balanced.
* **Eliminate sandbox introductions.** Never open a technical document with "In today's digital landscape..." or "Localization is an important process..." Start immediately with the technical purpose of the file.

### 3. Add Granular Specifics
* When documenting a bug in `memory.md` or `notes_and_lessons.md`, include the exact chaotic details of what broke. Do not write "The script failed to execute due to pathing issues." Write the exact error and the specific quirky reason it failed (e.g., "The python script choked because it expected `C13A537C` but the user's base graphics IPS was `CDD29127`, leading to a CRC mismatch.")
* Have an opinion. If a specific library or workflow in a-shell mini is frustrating, state it clearly. 

### 4. Vary Sentence Lengths (Burstiness)
* Read back your generated text. If every sentence is exactly two lines long, rewrite it. 
* Use short, punchy sentences. 
* Use fragments where appropriate for emphasis.
* Then use a longer, descriptive sentence when complex technical context requires it.

### 5. No Hallucinated Plaudits or "Hype Slop"
* Never refer to the project's own features as "stellar," "powerful," "beautiful," or "cutting-edge." Keep it entirely literal and objective.
* Do not attempt to formulate marketing copy in the `README.md`. It is a technical toolset for translating a PS1 game; it does not need to sound like an enterprise software rollout.

By understanding what makes AI text recognizable—the overly polite tone, the symmetrical paragraphs, the transition words, and the lack of specific, granular reality—we can consciously avoid these traps. The result is documentation that developers actually want to read: concise, opinionated, literal, and inherently human. 

These guidelines are now standard protocol for this workspace. Use them to self-police all future edits and updates to the core textual framework of this project.
